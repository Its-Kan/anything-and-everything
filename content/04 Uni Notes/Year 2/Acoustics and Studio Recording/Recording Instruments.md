When any recording instrument, there's always some things to consider:

1. Context: What is the recording for? Do you want a specific character in tone?
	- This informs the choices of mic, position and more.
2. Source: How does the instrument produce sound? Does it have any extraneous noises?
	- Remember: mics are stupid. They don't know what and what not to capture
	- Relate this to what character you want
	- Keep in mind mic selection, distance, reverb, and polar patterns

- For drums, replace drumheads if possible
- Eliminate any unwanted noises in the room

- Choosing specific instruments 
- Damping for drums to change

## Recording
### Guitars
#### Acoustic
The guitar is made up of multiple parts that produce noise
- The body produced low-mid "warmth"
- The sound hole emits a beam of a "boomy" low frequency range right out from the sound hole
- The strings provide "clarity" to the sound
- The neck gives a "shimmery" sound, as well as fret noise
- The picking area gives "attack"

In terms of recording, preparation is key; strings and tuning should be paid attention to. For mics, condenser/ribbon 

In terms of positions:
- Mike close
	- Start at the 12th fret at around 30cm.
	- Move towards the body for more body, towards the neck for more clarity  
- Common to mike in stereo, usually with XY at the position above, or a spaced pair on the neck and body.
#### Electric
We're miking an amplifier, and not the guitar itself. The player may have a certain sound, but it might sound different on the recording. Levels are also an issue, as guitars aren't suited for mic/line level inputs

Just so we get our terms straight, an amp is just the amplifier stage for the signal, and this sits on a cab, which actually has the speakers in it. Most amps are combo amps, which have both in a single box.
##### Straight from Amp
A cab has one or more speakers, which have a pretty complicated acoustic environment in the near field. 
- From the cone:
	- The centre of the cone is bright, while the edge is more warm/mellow
- Size (and volume) doesn't matter for the matter though, except when it starts distorting.
- Distance *does* matter however 
	 ![[Amp Distance.png]]

Mic position is crucial, so go prospecting for sounds just by moving the mic. If you play pink noise out of the mic, you can find any points of cancellation, and find a 

Any mic goes for amps. Bass doesn't matter too much as we'll cut it in mixing, but the upper mid range does. Common choices are:
- Dynamics: *SM57*, *MD421*, *e609*/*e906*
- Condensers - *C414*, *U87*
- Ribbons - *R1*, *X1R*

You can multi-mike amps too. This gives us more possibilities and options for sound.
- Comb filtering can help, by having two almost coincident mics, and then swivel one to change tone.
##### Through DI Boxes
Stands for direct injection/input. It's a box that accepts a guitar input, and then outputs both to an amp as well as a mic level output. Almost *always* a good idea, so you can have a dry signal if anything goes wrong.

The primary use is for re-amping. You can play it through a new amp later on, however you need a re-amp box, and pretty much does the opposite of a DI box. You can feed other things into a guitar amp too.
#### Bass
We want a focused, clear, even low end, but still with an intact high end. Distortion helps, which affects higher frequencies. Low frequencies are affected by the room. Play a chromatic scale upwards, and listen if there are any notes that are lost.

DI is a secret weapon, which is immune to room modes, and is commonly used combined with the amp signal, however it can be used on its own too. Amp miking similar to a guitar, but usually close in.
### Vocals
Pretty much one way we do this for anything. We want a very dry recording, in an acoustically "dead" room, or with a shield. We want the mic close around 15-40cm from the singer, with a large-diaphragm condenser. Make sure to add reverb to your singers headphones, so they can hear themselves better. Plosives (in 'p' and 'b' sounds) cause a very annoying bang noise. Pop shields help this.

If we focus on the voice as a source itself:
- Our voice projects forwards, but not evenly:
	- High frequencies project forwards, and downwards
### Piano
Another large, complex instrument with a massive frequency range. In the studio, mic close, however it's so physically big, we need multiple mics, even if it's mono in the mix. But, we'll record it as a stereo sound.

Let's consider the piano as a source:
- The piano lid projects an arc, where bright high frequencies are projected parallel to the hammers and keys, while warmer frequencies are at the low end 
- Height also matters, raising the mics gives a warm sound
#### Inside
We mike the piano with a spaced pair in front, facing the piano under the lid, one for the high strings and one for the low strings.
#### Outside
We can mike inside the piano for more clarity. We can do this under the lid above he strings, or over the hammers. Either Spaced (AB) or XY Pairs can works here. However if you're recording an upright, if possible, remove the front panel, as it might give an undesirable sound,
