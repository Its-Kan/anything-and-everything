## 1 Choice of Track
### 1.1 Track Description
Having experience with making covers, I had a wide range of unfinished projects that I could revitalise for the studio recording assessment. I looked for a song that I was able to play all the instruments for, only required some practise, and most importantly, meant something to me. I ended up choosing the song "Everblue"[^1] by the Japanese band "Omoinotake"[^2]. This song is the opening to the anime, "Blue Period"[^3] which follows the story of an aspiring artist finding his passion in his craft. This song reached out to me and my relationship with music, so it was able to fill my personal requirements for choice. 

I also decided to use a fan-made English translation of the lyrics by "Raegen"[^4], to ensure the vocals were more easily digestible. As the lyrics only went up to the first chorus, I had to write the rest myself. The original song was over the 2:30-3:30 time limit, so I had to make some modifications. I cut the final chorus, and re-instrumented the bridge to transition smoothly into a big-band inspired major 6/9 chord. 
### 1.2 Aesthetic Goals
Aesthetically, I wanted all of the elements in the mix to be distinct and clearly recognisable, as the piece itself is meant to resemble a thought-out, constructed painting, so I anticipated a lot of work in the mixing stage to make all the elements fit together.
### 1.3 Instrumentation and Arrangement
The song consists of vocals, piano, bass, drums, Clavinet and a brass section. The former 5 I was able to play myself, however sourcing an entire brass section would prove difficult. Instead, I got a friend who owned a Roland Aerophone AE-10[^5] EWI (electronic wind instrument), where I could record different on-board presets and layer it with MIDI data which fed into brass audio modelling software at the same time to emulate a full, rich brass section. I wouldn't make it front and centre in the mix, which would not distract from the other recorded elements.

## 2 Recording
### 2.1 Preparation
The recordings happened over multiple sessions in the University of York's Electronics Department Recording Studios[^6], with each session dedicated to a specific instrument. I had some help from friends to setup and hit record for me, however all mic placements and DAW organisation was my own. For recording, I used Cocos' Reaper [^7] as a DAW.

As I played all of the instruments myself, I had no choice but to record track-by-track. I used a bottom-up approach when choosing what order to record, starting the rhythm section (bass, drums and pianos), and then moving to the lead vocals and higher frequency instruments (EWI, clavinet and vocals). Ideally I would've used the demo tracks I created to use as guide tracks, but I ended up using a click track for the rhythm section recordings. I recorded multiple takes
 for each instrument.

### 2.2 Recording Each Element
#### 2.2.1 Bass
I used the bass and amp that were provided in the Electronics studios. Initially I recorded the bass using a DI box and an SM57, however when I listened to it back, there were some jarring resonances below the low G on the E string. I ended up recording it again through a re-amp box, however I took more care in the placement of the amp in the Live Room, by using a floor tom and finding where the room didn't resonate. I also added an extra C414 angled 45 degrees from the cone for more tone control.
#### 2.2.2 Drums
I used a slightly modified standard drum kit consisting of a kick drum, snare, hi-hat, 3 toms, 2 crashes, as well as a cowbell. Starting with the kick, I wanted a tight, airy sound so I positioned an AKG D112 nearer to the kick drum's sound hole. To support this, I added an SM57 on the beater-side of the kick, to capture the transients of the beater. I had two snare mics: an NT5 for the upper snare and then an SM57 capturing the chains on the underside. I positioned a C414 in-line with the hi-hat and snare, so the hi-hat can block the snare and reduce bleed. In terms of overheads, I had a spaced pair of NT5s, equidistant from the snare. I also wanted to capture more of the room acoustics, so I had a pair of C414's with a Figure-8 polar pattern facing each other placed in the corners of the room.
#### 2.2.3 Grand Piano
Recording the grand piano was a challenge due to its size, similar to the drums. I was able to use the grand piano in the university's Music Society practise rooms. I used two pairs of mics: two NT5 small-diaphragm condenser mics over the strings to capture the sharp transients of the hammers, as well as two elevated, spaced C414s in their cardioid setting to capture more body. Unfortunately there was a clicking sound whenever the pedal was pressed, and I couldn't easily fix, so it had to be fixed in the mixing stage.
#### 2.2.4 EWI
This was simply recorded with a C414 in cardioid placed directly in front of the speaker.
#### 2.2.5 Vocals
I used a Rode NT2-A with a pop-filter to handle plosives and an isolation shield to remove room acoustics. Background vocals were recorded with the same setup and an AT2020.
#### 2.2.6 Clavinet
This was recorded straight out of a Nord piano into the mixing desk.

## 3 Mixing
### 3.1 Preparation
I used Image-Line's FL Studio[^8] to mix, as I was more familiar with its workflow and plugins. Is mentioned previously, I wanted each element of the mix to be clear and distinct from each other, so heavily relied on visual mixing to plan out how I wanted the mix to sound like. Creating the graphic itself was very beneficial, as it helped me visualise if any elements clashed, and how I could address it either through panning, EQ or volume. Overall, I wanted the let frequencies to be as mono as possible to prevent "muddiness", and then have a wider stereo image going up the frequency spectrum. Visually, this made the mix look like an inverted cone.

Before I started mixing, I spliced together  the best takes for each instrument, as well as fixing any glaring timing errors. I made sure I cross-faded the spliced sections together to ensure there weren't any clicks or pops in the audio.
### 3.2 Mixing Each Element
#### 3.2.1 Bass
There were two aspects to the bass sound I wanted to accentuate in the mix: the finger picked low end and then a snappy pop in the mids, so I separated the high and low end to mix separately. For the low end "meat" of the bass, I wanted it to take up most of the low frequencies, however I found It clashed with the kick drum. I used a sidechain compressor to duck the low frequencies whenever the kick activated. For the pop sound, I boosted the most prominent parts of the sound, and then slightly it cut around it to exaggerate the sound, then added some saturation. I brought them together, and added  glue compression.
#### 3.2.2 Drums
The drums required a lot of EQ to lessen the bleed from the other sources, as well as to get each element distinct from one another. For instance, I added some cuts in the snares where the hi hat was most prominent to give it more room. I also had to clean-up some distortion from the beater mic whenever the floor tom was hit, likely due to the stand touching one of the legs. I ended up not using the room mics in the final mix, as they gave an airy quality to the kicks they I didn't like, and didn't add much to the mix itself. I sent all the drum tracks to a send track to apply glue compression and subtle EQ shaping in the sub and high frequencies.
#### 3.2.3 Grand Piano
In my visual mixing diagram, the piano fit between the horn section and drums in terms of frequency. I applied EQ accordingly to make room for these. I also added some saturation as well as boosting some mid frequencies to add more body. 
#### 3.2.4 EWI
The vocals are front and centre in the mix, so a lot of post-processing was done to make sure they fit In with instrumental. I decided to use parallel compression to preserve the overall dynamics, but  My signal chain was as follows: Autotune, subtractive and additive EQ, saturation, reverb. I found the vocals got hidden in the mix when all the instruments come together in the chorus, so I sent all the instruments to its own track, then had a side chain compressor duck the instrumental whenever the vocals were at a certain loudness.
#### 3.2.5 Vocals
The clavinet was simply just a low cut in EQ, as I wanted the second verse to feel less dense in terms of frequency. 
#### 3.2.6 Clavinet
The brass section was quite hard to mix as there were many elements playing at the same time. I panned each instance according to what line it was playing, and then routed them all to a send track to mix .
